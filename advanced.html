Geometry in the World: Midterm Project

The Geometry of Soccer Free Kicks

The mathematics of the "impossible" free kick, in full detail

By Aamod Paudel

A wall of players stands 9.15 m from the ball. The ball has to rise over them, fall under a bar 2.44 m high and land inside a 7.32 m gap. It has about one second. The best free-kick takers in the world solve this geometry problem with their feet. This project works out how.

The big picture

One question ties this whole project together

How do you get a ball through a window that a straight line cannot reach?

From the ball, the goal is not a big open net. Most of it is hidden behind the wall. What is left is a small window with three rules: go over the wall, come back under the bar, and stay inside the posts. A straight kick breaks at least one of those rules every time.

The catch: the only way through is a curve. Every idea below is one piece of the puzzle of building that curve, and at the end they come together into a final verdict on how to score.

01

Notice + Name

A ball that goes around what is in its way

I have watched hundreds of free kicks, and one thing never stopped bothering me. The wall is directly between the ball and the goal. A straight line from the ball to the goal goes right through the players' heads. Yet the best players keep scoring, and when you watch from behind the kicker the ball does not travel in a straight line at all. It bends.

I picked three famous free kicks because each one seems to bend in a different way. Press play, slow them down, and watch the shape the ball makes. All three clips are filmed from behind the kicker, so you see the ball the way the shooter does.

Up, over, down

Evidence: catching a curve on camera

I pulled the ball's path out of the video, frame by frame

The Ronaldo clip comes from a camera that barely moves. That let me take 60 frames (1/30 s apart), build an "empty" background from their median, and find the small white blob that changes from frame to frame: the ball. Stack those blobs and the invisible path appears.

Naming the pattern

A constrained curve

Every free kick is a curve that must pass through a narrow window: above the wall at 9.15 m, below the bar and inside the posts at the goal line. The geometry of the pitch sets the window. Physics decides which curves are possible. The player chooses one.

My mathematical questions

  1. Why can't a straight shot work? Can I prove the wall makes it impossible?
  2. What shape does the ball actually follow? My first guess was parabolas and maybe hyperbolas.
  3. Why do different kicks make different curves? Circle, dip, spiral, wobble.
  4. Does the geometry of the spot predict goals? If I know only where the ball is, can I estimate the chance of scoring?

Explain the Mathematics

Six pieces of one puzzle

2.1, piece 1 of 6

The angle of the goal, and the circles nobody draws

From where the ball sits, the two posts make an angle $\theta$. This is how much goal the shooter can actually "see". With the posts at $(\pm 3.66,\,0)$ and the ball at $(x,\,z)$:

$$\theta = \left|\arctan\frac{3.66 - x}{z} + \arctan\frac{3.66 + x}{z}\right|$$

Here is the surprise. By the inscribed angle theorem, every point on a circle through both posts sees the goal at the same angle, because they all look at the same chord. So the "equally good" spots on the pitch are arcs of circles, not straight lines.

Drag the ball. The highlighted circle goes through the ball and both posts. Slide the ball along it and $\theta$ does not change, even though the distance does.

2.2, piece 2 of 6

The wall's shadow: why a straight shot is impossible

Look from the side. A straight shot that just skims the top of the wall is a line through the ball and the wall's top. The wall (about 1.85 m tall, plus a 0.2 m jump) is at 9.15 m. The goal is at distance $d$. The two right triangles are similar, so:

$$\frac{h_{\text{goal}}}{d} = \frac{h_{\text{wall}}}{9.15} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h_{\text{goal}} = h_{\text{wall}}\cdot\frac{d}{9.15}$$

The wall casts a "shadow" on the goal that grows with distance. For the line to come in under the bar we need $2.05\cdot d/9.15 \le 2.33$ (the bar minus the ball's radius), so $d \le 10.4$ m. But the ball must be at least 9.15 m from the wall, which leaves almost no room.

Conclusion 1. From any normal free-kick spot, no straight line clears the wall and goes under the bar. The ball must curve downward. This answers my first question.

The ball must curve down. The first candidate force is gravity. 2.3 ↓

2.3, piece 3 of 6

Gravity's answer is a parabola, and it is not enough

Gravity alone bends the path downward. With no air, launch speed $v$ and launch angle $\alpha$, the height after travelling $x$ metres is a parabola:

$$y(x) = x\tan\alpha - \frac{g\,x^2}{2v^2\cos^2\alpha}$$

So my parabola guess was partly right. But the chart on the right shows the catch. Each point is one choice of speed and angle. The shaded region is every kick that clears the wall and dips under the bar. As the kick gets faster the parabola flattens and the window closes. A pure parabola only works if the ball is slow, which gives the keeper time.

Conclusion 2. Real fast free kicks drop more than gravity allows. Toggle the real flight (with air drag and topspin) and compare: the real path falls more steeply than it rises, so it is not symmetric and not a parabola at all.

Gravity fixes height only for slow kicks. Spin adds the missing force, and it also bends the ball sideways. 2.4 ↓

2.4, piece 4 of 6

Spin turns the path into a circle

A spinning ball drags air around with it. On one side the air speeds up, on the other it slows down, and the ball gets pushed sideways. This is the Magnus force. Its key feature is geometric: it always points at 90° to the direction the ball is moving.

$$F_{M} = \tfrac12\,\rho\,A\,C_L\,v^2 \;\perp\; \vec v$$

A force that is always perpendicular to the motion changes the direction, never the speed. Steady turning at a steady rate is exactly what defines a circle. Setting the Magnus force equal to the force needed for circular motion, $m v^2/R$, the $v^2$ cancels:

$$\frac{m v^2}{R} = \tfrac12\rho A C_L v^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R = \frac{2m}{\rho A C_L}$$

So the radius does not depend on speed at all, only on the ball and how much it spins. How far does the circle bend the ball away from the straight line? That is the sagitta of a chord of length $c$:

$$s = R - \sqrt{R^2 - \left(\tfrac{c}{2}\right)^2} \;\approx\; \frac{c^2}{8R}$$
Conclusion 3. Because the bend grows with the square of the distance, a curler that bends 1 m over 23 m bends about 2.3 m over 35 m. That is why long free kicks look so much more dramatic. This also explains the Ronaldo footage: the points after the wall fit a circle much better than a line or a parabola.

The circle assumed constant speed. Drag breaks that assumption. 2.5 ↓

2.5, piece 5 of 6

Roberto Carlos and the spiral

The circle argument assumed the ball keeps the same speed. In real air, drag slows it down. A smooth ball loses speed exponentially with the distance it travels:

$$v(s) = v_0\, e^{-s/L}, \qquad L = \frac{2m}{\rho A C_D}$$

But the spin hardly decays. The bending strength depends on the ratio of spin to speed, so the curvature $\kappa = 1/R$ grows as the ball slows:

$$\kappa(s) \propto \frac{\omega}{v(s)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \kappa(s) \approx \kappa_0\, e^{\,s/L}$$

A curve whose curvature keeps increasing is a spiral. The circles that best fit the path (the osculating circles) get smaller and smaller. That is the "tightening" I noticed in the Carlos clip. Physicists Dupeux, Le Goff, Quéré and Clanet showed exactly this in 2010 in The spinning ball spiral: Carlos hit the ball so hard, with so much spin and from so far, that the spiral part of the path became visible.

Conclusion 4. My parabola and hyperbola guess was wrong. Seen from above, a curling free kick is an arc of a spiral: almost a circle at first, then curling tighter. Seen from the side it is a drooping, asymmetric arch. Neither is a conic section.

Side note: when geometry loses

The knuckleball: almost no spin

Ronaldo is also famous for the opposite kick: hitting the ball with almost zero spin. With no steady Magnus force there is no circle. Instead the air peels off the ball unevenly and pushes it in small, random directions. Here are twelve kicks with the identical launch. Tiny differences in the airflow send them to different places, and the keeper cannot predict any of them. This is where tidy geometry turns into chaos.

2.6, piece 6 of 6

Does geometry predict goals? Every Messi free kick in La Liga

Last question: if I know only where the ball is, can geometry estimate the chance of scoring? I used StatsBomb's free open data, which logs every event in Messi's La Liga matches for Barcelona from 2004 to 2021. I pulled out all 1,322 direct free-kick shots (by Barcelona and their opponents) and measured two geometric features for each: the distance $d$ and the goal angle $\theta$ from 2.1.

A geometric model: logistic regression

I fitted a logistic regression, one of the simplest machine-learning models. It turns a straight-line score into a probability between 0 and 1 using the S-shaped logistic curve:

A positive angle coefficient means seeing more goal helps. A negative distance coefficient means being farther away hurts. The heat map shows the model's probability for every spot. Notice how its contours bend like the circles from 2.1: the model rediscovered the geometry.

How good is it? I tested it with 5-fold cross-validation (always predicting free kicks it had not seen). The AUC is about 0.67, where 0.5 is guessing and 1.0 is perfect. Geometry explains part of the story. The rest is the kicker, the wall, the keeper and luck.

✓

Putting the pieces together

The final verdict: how to score a free kick

To score, stop thinking in straight lines. Pick a spot with a wide angle, make the ball dip with gravity and topspin, make it bend around the wall with sidespin, and aim for the corner farthest from the keeper.

  1. Pick the spot. Every extra degree of goal you can see raises your odds of scoring by about 8%, and every metre closer raises them by about 12% (from the logistic regression: the odds multiply by about 1.08 per degree and 1.12 per metre). Central spots, on the bigger "same angle" circles, are best. Piece 1
  2. Forget the straight line. From 23 m, a straight kick that clears the wall reaches the goal about 5 m high, double the height of the bar. Piece 2
  3. Make it dip. Gravity alone only works below about 24 m/s, which is too slow to beat a keeper. Topspin adds the extra drop that brings a fast ball down under the bar. Piece 3
  4. Make it bend. Sidespin turns the path into part of a circle. The bend grows with the distance squared: about 1 m from 23 m, about 2.3 m from 35 m. Piece 4
  5. From far away, let the spiral surprise the keeper. As the ball slows down, the curve tightens at the very end, like Roberto Carlos in 1997. Piece 5
  6. Aim for the corners. 37 of Messi's 39 free-kick goals went into the outer parts of the goal, the places farthest from where the keeper stands. Piece 6

The catch

Even perfect geometry is not a guarantee. Messi, maybe the best free-kick taker ever, scored 39 of 420 (about 1 in 11), and the wall blocked 125 of them. In my simulation, only about 4% of random kicks from Ronaldo's spot score. Geometry tells you which curves are possible. Skill is hitting one of them, under pressure, in about one second.

The Shape of Possible

For my second creation I asked the simulator a question no player can answer: from this exact spot, what are all the ways to score? The program tries thousands of kicks (every mix of speed, angle, aim and spin), keeps only the goals and draws them all at once, from above and from behind the kicker.

The result is a picture of a mathematical object: the set of all successful curves. Its outline shows the window from Section 1. The empty bands are the wall's shadow and the keeper's reach.

My design choices and the maths behind them

  • Colour = spin direction. Pink curves bend left, blue curves bend right, and the brightness is the speed. You can see two "families" of solutions: curl it around the wall from one side, or the other.
  • Transparency adds up. Every path is drawn at 8% opacity, so bright regions are places many goals pass through. It is a density plot made of trajectories.
  • Two projections. The top view shows the horizontal circles and spirals from 2.4 and 2.5. The view from behind shows the window above the wall and below the bar from 2.2.

What I hope you wonder: if there are thousands of ways to score, why do so few players find even one?

04

Mathematical mindset

What I got wrong, and what it taught me

My first conjecture was wrong.

I expected parabolas and hyperbolas because those are the curves we study. The data and the physics said otherwise: the side view is an asymmetric arch and the top view is a spiral. Being wrong pointed me to the right question: what force is acting?

The parabola failed in a useful way.

The window chart in 2.3 showed that a parabola only works for slow kicks. That "failure" is the reason spin exists in the game. A model that breaks tells you where the interesting maths is.

My camera measurement has limits.

A video is a 2D shadow of a 3D path. My first tracking attempt picked up players' shirts as "balls". I had to restrict the search to a corridor and refine each point. I am confident about the bend, not about real-world metres.

The model is honest, not magic.

An AUC of 0.67 is modest. Geometry explains part of scoring, not all of it. Messi beat my geometric model by about two goals, and the wall still blocked 125 of his 420 attempts. Sometimes the most important finding is how much is left to explain.

Why this matters beyond football

The same mathematics shows up far from the pitch. Clubs now use geometric features (distance, angle, defenders in the way) in "expected goals" models to decide who to sign and where to shoot from. Ball designers test every new ball in wind tunnels, because a smoother ball wobbles more: the 2010 World Cup ball, the Jabulani, was famous for it. The same Magnus force makes spinning artillery shells drift sideways and pushes modern cargo ships fitted with spinning rotor sails, which saves fuel. And anyone with a phone camera can now measure a curve the way I did here. Geometry is not hiding in sport. It is the sport, played fast.
