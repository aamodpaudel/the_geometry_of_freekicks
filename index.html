The Geometry of Soccer Free Kicks

How do you kick a ball around a wall?

By Aamod Paudel

In a free kick, a line of players stands between the ball and the goal. The ball has to go over them, come back down under the crossbar, and land inside the goal. It does all of this in about one second. This page shows the geometry that makes it possible, using shapes you already know: angles, triangles, arches and circles.

The big picture

One question ties this whole project together

How do you get a ball through a window that a straight line cannot reach?

From the ball, the goal is not a big open net. Most of it is hidden behind the wall. What is left is a small window with three rules: go over the wall, come back under the bar, and stay inside the posts. A straight kick breaks at least one of those rules every time.

The catch: the only way through is a curve. Every idea below is one piece of the puzzle of building that curve, and at the end they come together into a final verdict on how to score.

01

Notice

The ball does not go in a straight line

Here is what I noticed. When a player takes a free kick, the wall of defenders stands right in the way. If you drew a straight line from the ball to the goal, it would hit someone's head. But great players still score. Watch from behind the kicker and you can see why: the ball curves.

I picked three famous free kicks. Each one curves in a different way. Watch them in slow motion and look at the shape the ball makes.

Up and down

Lionel Messi vs Liverpool, 2019, about 32 m away

The ball goes up over the wall, then drops fast into the top left corner. Think of a rainbow shape.

Sideways bend

Cristiano Ronaldo vs Spain, 2018 World Cup, about 23 m away

He kicks it toward the right side of the goal, and it bends left back in. Seen from above, it curves like part of a circle.

Tighter and tighter

Roberto Carlos vs France, 1997, about 35 m away

The ball looks like it will miss by a lot, then swerves back into the goal. The curve gets sharper near the end.

Naming it

A curve through a window

Every free kick has to pass through a small "window": above the wall, then under the crossbar and between the posts. The kicker's job is to pick a curve that fits through it.

02

Explain the maths

Six pieces of one puzzle

1. Seeing the goal 2. The wall's shadow 3. Gravity's arch 4. Spin makes a circle 5. The spiral 6. Messi's data

Piece 1 of 6: where to kick from

How much goal can you see? It's an angle.

Stand at the ball and point at the left post, then at the right post. The space between your two arms is an angle. A bigger angle means you can see more goal to aim at.

  • Right in front of the goal: big angle. Easy.
  • Far away, or off to the side: small angle. Hard.
The cool part. Every point on the same circle through both posts sees the goal at exactly the same angle. This is a real geometry rule called the inscribed angle theorem. So "equally good" spots on the pitch are not in a straight line. They sit on a circle.

Try it: drag the white ball. The yellow circle passes through the ball and both posts. Move the ball along that circle and the angle stays the same.

So far: the spot decides how big the target is. Next: from any spot, the wall is still in the way. Why not just kick over it in a straight line? Piece 2 ↓

Piece 2 of 6: why not straight

The wall makes a "shadow", so a straight kick can't work

Imagine shining a flashlight from the ball. The wall casts a shadow toward the goal. A straight kick that just clears the top of the wall keeps rising in a straight line. By the time it reaches the goal, it is way too high.

How high? The two triangles in the picture are similar (same shape, different size). So:

height at the goal = wall height × distance to goaldistance to wall

Example: the wall is about 2 m tall (with a jump) and 9.15 m away. The goal is 23 m away, which is about 2.5 times farther. So the ball arrives about 2.5 × 2 = 5 m high. The crossbar is only 2.44 m. Miss!

So: from a normal free-kick spot, a straight line cannot go over the wall and under the bar. The ball has to curve down.

So far: the ball must curve down. Next: what can bend a ball downward? Gravity. Piece 3 ↓

Piece 3 of 6: getting over and down

Gravity bends the ball into an arch (a parabola)

Throw anything and it travels in an arch: up, then down. In maths this arch shape is a parabola, the same shape as the graph of $y = -x^2$. Gravity is what pulls the ball back down, so gravity gives us the "curve down" we need.

But there is a catch. Play with the sliders:

  • Kick slowly and the arch is tall and round. It can clear the wall and drop in. But a slow ball is easy for the goalkeeper.
  • Kick hard and the arch gets flat. Now it either hits the wall or flies over the bar.
So: look at the green zone in the chart. It is every kick that works. Above about 24 m/s (86 km/h) the green zone disappears. Gravity alone is not enough for a fast free kick. Players need something extra: spin.

So far: gravity handles up and down, but only for slow kicks. Next: spin, which also lets the ball go around the wall. Piece 4 ↓

Piece 4 of 6: getting around

Spin pushes the ball sideways, in a circle

When a ball spins, it drags the air around with it. Air on one side moves faster than the other, so the ball gets pushed sideways. This is called the Magnus effect. It is the same reason a spinning ping-pong ball or baseball curves.

Here is the geometry. The push is always at a right angle (90°) to the direction the ball is going. Think of swinging a ball on a string: the string always pulls at 90° to the motion, and the ball moves in a circle. Spin does the same thing, so seen from above, the ball travels along part of a big circle.

how far it bends ≈ distance × distance8 × radius of the circle

Because distance is multiplied by itself, doubling the distance makes the bend 4 times bigger. That is why long free kicks look so much more curvy.

Remember the dots from Ronaldo's video? They fit a circle best. Now we know why: spin makes circles.

So far: spin makes a circle if the speed stays the same. Next: what happens when the air slows the ball down? Piece 5 ↓

Piece 5 of 6: kicking from far away

Roberto Carlos: a circle that keeps shrinking (a spiral)

The circle idea works if the ball keeps the same speed. But air slows the ball down. The spin stays strong, so as the ball slows, the spin wins more and more, and the ball turns more sharply.

A curve that turns tighter and tighter is a spiral, like a snail shell. That is why Carlos's kick looked like it "suddenly" bent at the end. In 2010, four scientists wrote a whole research paper about this kick called The spinning ball spiral.

  • The labels show the size of the circle the ball is following at each moment.
  • Watch the circle get smaller as the ball slows down. Tick the box to keep it flying and see the full spiral.
So my first guess was wrong. I thought free kicks would be parabolas or hyperbolas. From the side it is a squashed arch, and from above it is a piece of a spiral.

Piece 6 of 6: testing it on real games

Does the spot predict a goal? I checked every Messi free kick

I downloaded free match data from a company called StatsBomb. It lists every shot in Messi's Spanish league games from 2004 to 2021. That gave me 1,322 free kicks to study, including all of Messi's.

Teaching a computer the geometry

I gave a computer just two numbers for every free kick:

  • Distance to the goal
  • Angle of goal you can see (from Idea 1)

Then I let it learn a rule that turns those two numbers into a chance of scoring. This is a simple kind of machine learning called logistic regression. The rule it learned is what you would expect: closer is better and more angle is better.

The map on the right colours every spot by that chance. Notice the coloured bands are curved like the circles from Idea 1. The computer found the geometry by itself.

How good is it? Show the computer one goal and one miss, and ask "which one was the goal?" It picks right about 67% of the time. A coin flip would get 50%. So the spot matters, but it is not everything. The kicker's skill, the wall and the keeper matter too.
Messi vs the computer. From the spots Messi kicked from, the computer expected about 37 goals. Messi scored 39. He beats the geometry, but only a little.

Putting the pieces together

The final verdict: how to score a free kick

To score, stop thinking in straight lines. Pick a spot with a wide angle, make the ball dip with gravity and topspin, make it bend around the wall with sidespin, and aim for the corner farthest from the keeper.

  1. Pick the spot. Every extra degree of goal you can see raises your odds of scoring by about 8%, and every metre closer raises them by about 12%. Central spots, on the bigger "same angle" circles, are best. Piece 1
  2. Forget the straight line. From 23 m, a straight kick that clears the wall reaches the goal about 5 m high, double the height of the bar. Piece 2
  3. Make it dip. Gravity alone only works below about 24 m/s, which is too slow to beat a keeper. Topspin adds the extra drop that brings a fast ball down under the bar. Piece 3
  4. Make it bend. Sidespin turns the path into part of a circle. The bend grows with the distance squared: about 1 m from 23 m, about 2.3 m from 35 m. Piece 4
  5. From far away, let the spiral surprise the keeper. As the ball slows down, the curve tightens at the very end, like Roberto Carlos in 1997. Piece 5
  6. Aim for the corners. 37 of Messi's 39 free-kick goals went into the outer parts of the goal, the places farthest from where the keeper stands. Piece 6

The catch

Even perfect geometry is not a guarantee. Messi, maybe the best free-kick taker ever, scored 39 of 420 (about 1 in 11), and the wall blocked 125 of them. In my simulation, only about 4% of random kicks from Ronaldo's spot score. Geometry tells you which curves are possible. Skill is hitting one of them, under pressure, in about one second.

The Shape of Possible

I asked my game a question no player can answer: from this spot, what are all the ways to score? The computer tries thousands of random kicks, throws away the misses, and draws every goal on top of each other.

The picture that appears is the shape of every possible goal. It is the final verdict drawn by a computer: the bright paths dip under the bar, bend around the wall and head for a corner. The empty gaps are where the wall and the goalkeeper block the way.

Why I made it this way

  • Pink and blue. Pink paths bend left and blue paths bend right. You can see two groups: go around the wall on one side, or the other.
  • See-through lines. Each path is very faint, so bright areas are where lots of goals pass.
  • Two views. From above you see the circles (Idea 4). From behind you see the window over the wall and under the bar (Idea 2).
  • Farther = fewer. From 23 m about 4% of random kicks score. From 35 m it drops below 1%. The geometry gets harder with distance.

What I hope you wonder: if there are thousands of ways to score, why do so few players find even one?

04

What I learned

Mistakes that helped me

My first guess was wrong.

I thought the ball would follow a parabola or a hyperbola, because those are the curves we learn about. It turned out to be an uneven arch from the side and a spiral from above. Being wrong made me ask a better question: what is pushing the ball?

The simple model broke, and that was useful.

Gravity alone (the parabola) only works for slow kicks. That "failure" explained why players spin the ball at all.

Measuring a video is tricky.

My first try at finding the ball also picked up white shirts. I had to search only near the ball's path. A video is a flat picture of a 3D world, so I trust the shape of the curve more than exact distances.

Maths explains a lot, not everything.

The spot predicts goals better than a coin flip, but not perfectly. Skill, the wall and luck still matter. Messi only beat the geometry by about 2 goals.

Words to know

Angle

How far apart two lines open from a point. Here: how much goal you can see from the ball.

Similar triangles

Triangles with the same shape but different sizes. Their sides grow by the same scale factor.

Parabola

The U-shaped (or arch-shaped) curve of a thrown object, like the graph of y = x².

Radius

The distance from the centre of a circle to its edge. A big radius means a gentle curve.

Inscribed angle theorem

All points on a circle "see" the same chord (here, the goal) at the same angle.

Magnus effect

A spinning ball gets pushed sideways by the air. It makes the ball curve.

Spiral

A curve that keeps turning tighter, like a snail shell.

Logistic regression

A simple computer method that turns numbers into a chance between 0% and 100%.

Why this matters

Real soccer clubs use this same geometry, like distance and angle to the goal, to decide where players should shoot from. Ball makers test new balls because a smoother ball wobbles more. And the same spin effect is used to help push some modern cargo ships, which saves fuel. Geometry is not only in textbooks. It is in every curving free kick.
